VC method, revenue multiples, scenario weighting and the gap between headline and structured price.

Why venture valuation is different

Early-stage companies have negative cash flows, short histories and wide outcome ranges. A DCF is dominated by its terminal value and by assumptions no one can observe. So venture investors value from the exit backward, cross-check against market multiples, and weight scenarios explicitly. Growth equity sits in between: more data and more structure, so a base-case underwriting with downside protection matters more.

The VC method

Exit value = Exit-year revenue (or ARR) × Exit multiple Max post-money = Exit value × (1 − expected dilution) ÷ Target MOIC Max pre-money = Max post-money − Investment

The target multiple is high because it has to pay for the deals that fail. Typical targets fall as stage rises, from 10x or more at seed to 3–5x at growth, but they vary by fund.

Market multiples

For software, the common yardstick is EV / NTM revenue (or EV / ARR), read against growth. A growth-adjusted multiple (EV/NTM revenue ÷ NTM growth) helps compare companies with different growth, but do not extrapolate it far beyond the peer set's growth range, and remember that durability and margins matter as much as the growth rate.

Equity value vs. enterprise value

Enterprise value ≈ Equity value − Cash + Debt Post-money is an equity value, and it includes the new cash from the round.

Comparing a post-money valuation directly to public EV multiples overstates the private multiple when the company holds a lot of cash.

Private marks and headline prices

Last-round prices are reference points, not fair value. Structure changes value: a high headline price with a senior 2x preference, participation or an IPO ratchet is not the same as a clean price. In a downside exit, structure moves value from common to preferred. When you cite a peer's valuation, ask what terms sat behind it.

Scenario weighting

Write down bear, base and bull cases with probabilities and compute expected proceeds, including the effect of your liquidation preference in the bear case. If the deal only works in the bull case, say so.

Worked example · Cobalt Ledger

VC method. ARR of $12.0M compounding at 49% for five years reaches about $88M. At 8x ARR, exit value is about $705M. With 27.4% expected dilution and a 5x target:

Max post-money = $705M × 72.6% ÷ 5 ≈ $102M

The proposed price is a $200M post-money. At that price the base case returns about 2.6x (21% IRR), short of a 5x target.

Practice check

Five questions

Question 1 of 5
Expected exit value is $1.0B, target 10x, no dilution. What is the maximum post-money? With 30% dilution?
$1.0B ÷ 10 = $100M. Retaining 70% through exit: $1.0B × 0.7 ÷ 10 = $70M.
Question 2 of 5
Why can a growth-adjusted multiple mislead?
A company growing 80% is not worth twice a 40% grower's multiple if that growth will not last.
Question 3 of 5
Company X raised at a $1.0B headline price with a senior 2x participating preference. Company Y raised at a clean $800M. Which is true?
Structure is part of price. Compare like for like by modeling the waterfall.
Question 4 of 5
A company has a $200M post-money, $60M of cash after the round and no debt. What is its approximate enterprise value?
EV ≈ equity value − cash + debt = 200 − 60 = $140M.
Question 5 of 5
What is the main problem with a DCF for a seed-stage company?
A DCF can still discipline a growth-stage case, but early on it mostly reflects assumptions.

AI watch-outs for this topic

Where AI output goes wrong here

  • Comparing post-money (equity value, including new cash) with public EV multiples.
  • Mixing NTM and LTM multiples, or ARR and revenue, in one comp set.
  • Citing a peer's headline valuation without the terms behind it.
  • Scenario probabilities that do not sum to 100%, or a bear case that ignores the preference.
  • Stating an "industry average multiple" with no peer set or date.
