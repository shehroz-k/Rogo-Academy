Fund Economics & Portfolio Construction

Fees, carry, reserves, TVPI vs. DPI and why every check must be able to return the fund.

How a fund works

Limited partners (LPs) commit capital. The general partner (GP) calls it over an investment period of about five years, then harvests over the remaining life, typically ten years with extensions. Common terms:

  • Management fee: often around 2% of commitments a year during the investment period, stepping down afterward. Over the fund's life, fees can total 15–20% of commitments.
  • Carried interest: commonly 20% of profits after LPs get their capital back. Some funds charge more; hurdles are more common in private equity than in venture.
  • Recycling: reinvesting early proceeds to put more of the commitment to work.
Investable capital = Commitments − Lifetime fees (+ recycling)

Portfolio construction

A strategy sets the number of companies, the initial check size, the target ownership and the reserves held for follow-ons. Many early-stage funds reserve 40–60% of investable capital. Reserves let the fund defend ownership in winners through pro rata, but money spent defending average companies is money not spent on the outliers.

Measuring performance

DPI = Distributions ÷ Paid-in capital (cash returned: fact) RVPI = Residual value (NAV) ÷ Paid-in capital (unrealized: the GP's mark) TVPI = DPI + RVPI IRR = The discount rate that sets the dated cash flows' NPV to zero

Gross vs. net: gross returns are on invested capital before fees and carry. Net returns are what LPs actually receive. The J-curve: early net returns are negative, because fees are paid and losers are written down before winners mature.

Why every check must be able to return the fund

Because outcomes follow a power law, a fund typically needs one or a few companies to return the whole fund. Work backward: given the ownership you will have at exit, how big must the exit be? If that number is implausible for the market, the deal does not fit the fund, however good the company.

Worked example · Cobalt Ledger

A $500M fund. Fees of 2% for five years and 1.5% for five years total $87.5M (17.5%), leaving $412.5M investable.

Target 3.0x net TVPI. LPs need $1.5B back. With 20% carry on profits above $500M:

G − 20% × (G − 500) = 1,500 → G = $1.75B of gross proceeds Gross multiple on invested capital = 1,750 ÷ 412.5 = 4.2x

Fund-returner math for Cobalt Ledger. At 14.5% exit ownership, returning the $500M fund requires a $3.4B exit. The base case is about $700M. Cobalt can be a good investment without returning the fund, but the partnership should decide that knowingly.

Reading a fund report. Paid-in $400M, distributions $240M, NAV $520M: DPI 0.60x, RVPI 1.30x, TVPI 1.90x. Two-thirds of the value is still a mark.

Practice check

Five questions

Question 1 of 5
Paid-in $100M, distributions $50M, NAV $150M. What are TVPI and DPI?
TVPI = (50 + 150) ÷ 100 = 2.0x. DPI = 50 ÷ 100 = 0.5x.
Question 2 of 5
Why is net TVPI lower than gross MOIC on invested capital?
LPs pay fees on commitments and give up carry, so they receive less than the portfolio's gross multiple.
Question 3 of 5
A $300M fund expects 10% average exit ownership. What exit returns the fund from one company?
$300M ÷ 10% = $3.0B.
Question 4 of 5
What is the main reason to hold reserves?
Reserves concentrate capital in winners. The discipline is not to spend them propping up average companies.
Question 5 of 5
What explains the J-curve?
Net returns dip early, then rise as winners are marked up and eventually distributed.

AI watch-outs for this topic

Where AI output goes wrong here

  • Confusing TVPI with DPI, or presenting unrealized marks as returns.
  • Gross vs. net mislabeled, especially in fund comparisons.
  • Fund-returner math that silently uses investable capital instead of fund size, or the reverse.
  • IRR quoted without dates, or on a subset of cash flows.
  • Industry "average" fee and carry terms stated as facts for a specific fund.
