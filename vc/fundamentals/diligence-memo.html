Size the market bottom-up

TAM = Target accounts × Annual contract value (ACV) SAM = TAM × Share of accounts the product can serve today SOM = SAM × Share the company can realistically win

Then triangulate with any top-down figure, and explain the gap. A top-down number usually defines the market more broadly, for example by including payments revenue or a wider customer segment.

Reference calls that produce evidence

  • Ask for examples, not adjectives: "What did it replace? What changed in your close timeline, in days?"
  • Ask what nearly made them churn, and what they would cut first in a budget squeeze.
  • Ask who else they evaluated and why they chose this product.
  • Use backchannel references the founder did not provide, within firm policy.

The investment memo

A memo exists so a partner can challenge your thinking quickly. Lead with the recommendation and terms. Keep three things visibly separate: verified data, management claims and our underwriting assumptions.

  1. Recommendation, round terms and our check
  2. Investment thesis: three reasons, each with evidence
  3. Company, product and how it wins
  4. Market: bottom-up sizing and why now
  5. Traction and unit economics, on our definitions
  6. Team
  7. Competition
  8. Key risks and mitigants
  9. Thesis falsifiers: what would prove us wrong, and how we would know
  10. Valuation, scenarios and returns
  11. Diligence completed, open items and the ask
Worked example · Cobalt Ledger

Bottom-up market (illustrative inputs). 45,000 US companies with $50M–$1B of revenue × a $30K ACV gives a $1.35B TAM. If 60% run an ERP Cobalt integrates with today, SAM is $810M. The third-party estimate of $4.2B likely includes payment revenue and a broader segment. Say so, rather than quoting the bigger number.

Key risks to resolve before IC. ARR on a live-contract basis; retention on an all-customer definition (116%, not 118%); the path from a 2.0x burn multiple to below 1.5x; and renewal dates for the top 10 customers (31% of ARR).

Thesis falsifiers. Win rates against ERP-bundled modules fall below one in three, or GRR drops below 85% as the base broadens.

Drill: red-flag triage

Classify each diligence finding. The explanation appears after you choose.

Top 10 customers are 31% of ARR; the largest is 7%.
Moderate concentration for a $12M ARR company. Diligence renewal dates and the health of the top accounts, but it is not disqualifying.
The largest customer is 38% of ARR, and its contract renews in four months.
A single renewal could cut ARR by more than a third. Speak to the customer, or structure around it, before IC.
ARR includes $1.1M of signed contracts not yet live, some going live more than six months out.
About 9% of ARR is not yet earning revenue. Restate ARR on a live basis and treat signed-not-live separately.
The founder cannot produce a clean cap table; two early option grants were never board-approved.
A legal cleanup item that must be fixed before closing. It also tells you something about operating rigor.
Gross margin fell from 80% to 78% after onboarding staff were moved into cost of revenue.
An explained, reasonable classification change. Note it, and compare margins on a consistent basis.
Three reference customers praise the product, but none can name a metric it improved.
Satisfaction without measurable impact is a pricing-power and churn risk. Push for specific outcomes in more calls.
Burn multiple improved from 3.1x to 2.0x over four quarters.
A positive trend. Diligence what drove it, and whether it continues after the new hiring plan.
Core IP was written by a contractor who never signed an IP assignment.
The company may not own its core technology. Get an assignment signed before closing.

Investment memo draftUse your own data room
Using only the attached materials (data room extracts, my diligence notes and our returns model), draft an investment memo for [company] in this structure:
1. Recommendation, round terms and our check
2. Thesis: three reasons, each with evidence and a source
3. Company and product
4. Market: bottom-up sizing (accounts × ACV) with stated assumptions, compared with any top-down figure
5. Traction and unit economics on our definitions (state where the company's definitions differ)
6. Team
7. Competition
8. Key risks and mitigants
9. Thesis falsifiers: what would prove us wrong and how we would know
10. Valuation, scenarios and returns (from the attached model only)
11. Diligence completed, open items and asks
Label every statement as [Verified], [Management] or [Our assumption]. Cite document and page for every figure. Do not add outside data. List anything you could not support.
Practice check

Five questions

Question 1 of 5
Why is bottom-up market sizing preferred to a top-down figure?
Accounts × ACV makes every assumption explicit and checkable. Use top-down figures to triangulate.
Question 2 of 5
Which reference-call question produces the best evidence?
Specific, measurable before-and-after answers are evidence. Adjectives are not.
Question 3 of 5
What is a thesis falsifier?
Naming falsifiers in advance keeps the portfolio review honest after the investment.
Question 4 of 5
In a memo, why separate verified data, management claims and our assumptions?
Partners challenge assumptions differently from facts. Blending them hides the real risk.
Question 5 of 5
ARR includes signed contracts that are not yet live. What is the right treatment?
Signed-not-live contracts can slip or cancel. Keep them visible, but separate.

AI watch-outs for this topic

Where AI output goes wrong here

  • Top-down market sizes with no definition, or a definition that does not match the product.
  • Management claims rewritten as verified facts in a first-draft memo.
  • Reference call summaries that turn adjectives into evidence.
  • Missing contrary evidence, because the model writes toward the conclusion you asked for.
  • Diligence "completed" lists that include items never actually performed.
